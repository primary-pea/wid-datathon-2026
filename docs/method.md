# Method, on one page

**Question.** Across 49 sub-Saharan African countries, does the number of nutrition policies a government has on the books move with the anaemia measured in its women aged 15–49?

**The panel.** One table, `derived/ssa_panel.csv`: 49 countries × 2000–2025, 1,274 rows × 174 columns, assembled from twelve sources by one guarded script (`scaffold/00_assemble_panel.py`) that refuses duplicate keys, undeclared gaps and an incomplete grid. Every input is pinned by SHA-256; the panel's own hash is the data version (`afe63e46c26b`). 24 tests run on every rebuild (25 collected; one, the retired R replica, is skipped by design).

**Three questions, three tools.**

1. *Descriptive — where is it worst and which way is it moving.* The WHO anaemia series (via FAOSTAT), unweighted and population-weighted regional means, and the team's concern score over four women's indicators (flag 0 improved / 1 started in the worst quarter / 2 worsened, plus the percentile; equal weights; 500 random weightings for rank stability).
2. *Association — within each country over time.* Two-way fixed effects: anaemia in country *i* and year *t* on the number of anaemia-cluster policies active three years earlier, with log GDP per capita (constant 2015 US$), country effects and year effects; standard errors clustered by country, wild cluster bootstrap p-values alongside. The dose counts dated **policies** from the WHO GIFNA registry whose tokens match the anaemia cluster (iron, folate, MMS; rules in `scaffold/inputs/gifna_cluster_rules.csv`).
3. *Context — what explains the level differences.* Anaemia on sub-region, log income, malaria incidence, fertility, urban share, basic sanitation, terrain ruggedness, tropical share and distance to coast, with year effects (R² 0.81, 45 countries), against income-plus-year alone (R² 0.06). Countries are ranked by their residual; the two eight-country tails are compared on their policy records.

**The guards on the main estimate**, each a row of the ladder in `scaffold/results/fe_sensitivity.csv`: two- and five-year lags; dropping thinly observed countries; income in current US$; a **lead test** with policies three years in the future; country-specific trends; first differences, five-year differences and a lagged outcome; one extra control at a time (malaria, fertility, urban share, HIV, sanitation, employment, deworming coverage); sub-samples where an extra control exists (food insecurity, the FIES gender gap, diet affordability, hospital beds, health spending). Then **336 pre-specified specifications** (seven doses × three lags × two income measures × with/without context × four estimators), **event studies** around the first dated anaemia policy and the wheat-flour mandate with three estimators built for staggered adoption, and **leave-one-country-out** prediction against a ladder of six models.

**What came out.** The main estimate is −0.21 points of anaemia per policy, 95% interval −0.62 to +0.20 (p = 0.32): a null. The equivalence test bounds it: effects at or beyond 0.60 points per policy are ruled out (TOST p = 0.03), with 80% power at 0.59. Policies three years in the future "predict" current anaemia (−0.34, p = 0.03) — the signature of policy following the trend, not leading it; with country trends the estimate is −0.02. Of 336 specifications, 22 exclude zero, 15 one way and 7 the other; the median is −0.02 per within-country standard deviation of the dose. Adding policy improves the held-out forecast in 22 of 49 countries (binomial p = 0.57). The two tails of the context ranking have indistinguishable policy records (Mann-Whitney p = 0.11).

**How to read a null with a bound.** The data cannot show that the policies did nothing; they show that a large effect of *counted policy documents* is not there, and they say why the count is the wrong instrument: the registry records what is written, three in four programme records carry no date, none records coverage, and only 33 of the 49 countries have a haemoglobin survey in the window. Until delivery is recorded, nobody can say which policies work.
